A single 360° panorama lets a viewer stand in one spot and look anywhere; it
does not let them walk toward the window or step past the sofa. That's the
whole limitation of a virtual tour built from panoramas - one shot per room,
and the room stops being explorable at the doorway. I've been developing a way
past that limitation for Virtuard, a villa-rental platform whose listings are
built entirely from 360° tours: one panorama per room, so a viewer can rotate
from the shooting point but not move through the space.

The obvious fix is monocular depth estimation: feed the panorama through a
model that predicts a depth value for every pixel, and you get a 3D surface
instead of a flat image. It works, it's fast, and it invents nothing that
wasn't photographed - but it only gets you so far. Move the virtual camera
more than about a metre from where the photograph was actually taken and you
start hitting black holes: areas the original camera never saw, because they
were behind a sofa, around a corner, or simply outside its field of view from
that one point. A single viewpoint only ever describes what was visible from
that viewpoint.

## The insight hiding in the data

The fix I ended up building doesn't start from a smarter depth model. It
starts from something already sitting in the data: tours frequently already
contain more than one panorama of the same room, shot from different points,
because whoever captured the tour walked in, took a shot, moved a few metres,
and took another - a "Living Room" and a "Living Room 2". Each of those
panoramas sees exactly what the other one can't. The corner that's a black
hole in the first shot is often plainly visible from the second. If I can work
out that two panoramas belong to the same physical room and align them
correctly, I can fuse them, and the blind spots get filled with geometry that
was genuinely photographed - not guessed.

That's the core idea: multi-view fusion. Everything else in this piece is
either the case for choosing it over the alternatives, or the mechanics of
making it actually work.

## Four ways to fill in a room, and why I picked one

I evaluated four approaches before settling on multi-view fusion, and each has
a real trade-off worth stating plainly rather than glossing over.

**Monocular depth from a single panorama** is fast and invents nothing beyond
what the depth model infers from a single image, but it tops out at roughly a
metre of usable movement before the black holes dominate.

**Gaussian splatting from a walk-through video** produces by far the best
quality of the four - it's the closest thing to an actual 3D capture of the
room. But it needs a dedicated video walk-through shot for every property,
which doesn't scale against an existing catalogue of tours that were never
filmed that way.

**Generative 3D reconstruction** - feeding the existing panorama into a model
that hallucinates plausible 3D structure for the parts it can't see - reuses
the photos that already exist and produces results that look excellent at
first glance. I set it aside for two reasons, both worth stating rather than
hand-waving. First, it invents things: rooms, windows, even a pool that isn't
there. That's disqualifying on a rental listing, where the person booking is
deciding based on what they believe they saw - showing them square footage or
a view that doesn't exist isn't a rendering quirk, it's a promise the property
can't keep. Second, the leading open generative pipelines for this kind of
reconstruction lean on components released under non-commercial licences,
which rules them out of a commercial product without negotiating a separate
agreement - a real constraint, independent of how good the output looks.

**Multi-view fusion** - my own approach, and the subject of the rest of this
piece - reuses the panoramas that already exist, same as the generative
approach, but instead of inventing what it can't see, it looks for a second
real photograph that already saw it.

## Stage one: do these two panoramas actually show the same room?

Two panoramas named "Living Room" and "Living Room 2" are not necessarily the
same room - and even when they are, fusing two panoramas of different rooms
produces geometry that's confidently wrong rather than honestly incomplete. So
before any alignment happens, the pipeline has to answer a narrower question:
do these two images actually show the same physical space?

I project each panorama onto the six faces of a cube - the standard way to
turn a spherical image into something a conventional 2D feature matcher can
operate on - and run a learned dense feature matcher between the corresponding
faces of the two panoramas. That gives a set of candidate correspondences:
points the matcher believes show the same physical location in both images.
Candidate correspondences are cheap and frequently wrong, so the next step is
a robust geometric filter - fundamental-matrix estimation verified with
MAGSAC - that keeps only the correspondences consistent with a single,
physically coherent camera geometry between the two shots. The number of
matches that survive this filter is the score for the pair; below a
threshold, I reject it.

That score is doing real work. One living-room pair I measured scored 598
surviving matches - unambiguously the same room. A pair of entrance panoramas,
despite the similar names an operator had given them, scored 13 to 39 matches
across three attempts and was correctly rejected. Same naming pattern,
opposite answer, and the geometry is what tells them apart, not the label.

One detail that surprised me enough to note it: feature-matcher weights
trained on outdoor scenes outperformed the indoor-trained weights on these
villa interiors. I don't have a tidy explanation for that beyond noting that
it's the kind of assumption - "indoor weights for indoor scenes" - that's
worth actually testing rather than taking for granted.

## Stage two: align and fuse

Once a pair passes verification, the second stage turns the shared
correspondences into an actual merge. I re-render the two views at angles
chosen so each correspondence lands on a well-conditioned 3D point - feature
matches taken at a glancing angle produce noisy depth, so the re-rendering
step exists purely to give the alignment better-conditioned geometry to work
with. From there I recover scale, rotation and translation between the two
point clouds in one shot with a robust estimator - RANSAC combined with
Umeyama's method - and refine the result with ICP (Iterative Closest Point)
against the overlapping surfaces.

The last step is the one that actually matters for output quality: I only add
points from the second panorama that are more than 10 cm away from a surface
the first panorama already covers. That threshold is what keeps the merge
additive rather than destructive - it contributes genuinely new geometry from
the blind spots, and it never lets the second panorama's noisier
reconstruction overwrite or duplicate a surface the first one already
resolved cleanly.

## Why scale is the hard part

Everything above sounds mechanical once it's laid out, but the actual hard
problem underneath it is scale, and it's worth explaining why.

Monocular depth estimation is scale-invariant by construction: the model
predicts relative depth, and every panorama comes out at its own arbitrary
size, unrelated to metres, unrelated to any other panorama's scale. Two
panoramas of the same living room can - and in practice do - come out of the
depth model at two different, internally-consistent-but-mutually-incompatible
scales. Recovering the true metric scale during alignment isn't a
nice-to-have; without it, ICP will happily converge two point clouds into a
geometrically plausible but physically wrong merge.

It gets worse than "the model doesn't tell you the scale." I worked out that
once the per-face depth maps for a single panorama are merged into one
coherent spherical depth map, the absolute scale becomes mathematically
unrecoverable from that panorama alone - the merge step solves a system
defined over log-depth gradients between adjacent faces, and a global scale
factor is exactly the kind of quantity that leaves no trace in a system of
gradients: multiply every depth by the same constant and every gradient in the
system is unchanged. The scale has to come from outside that panorama - from
cross-referencing it against something else, which is exactly what the
two-panorama fusion, and ultimately a real floor plan with real measurements,
provides. That's why a floor-plan and scale-calibration interface - a way for
the platform to collect real, measured floor plans per property - is worth
building even though it lives outside the reconstruction pipeline itself: it's
the one input that can give this whole approach an anchor to true metric
scale.

- it was outside the first panorama's field of view entirely, and only the
second panorama's vantage point had ever photographed it. Typical residual
misalignment on the surfaces the two panoramas share, after ICP refinement,
is about 8 cm.

## Knowing when not to trust itself

The part of this I'd defend hardest isn't the fusion - it's that the pipeline
checks its own work. It computes two independent estimates of the scale
factor between a panorama pair and compares them, because if the two
estimates disagree, that disagreement is real information about how much to
trust the merge, not noise to average away.

On the living room above, the two estimates agreed within 3% - a reliable
merge. On one hallway pair, they diverged by 25%; the fusion still measurably
improved the unreconstructed area, from 32% down to 23%, but with real
uncertainty attached to the result, and the pipeline reports that uncertainty
rather than presenting the number as clean. On a guest-room pair, the two
estimates diverged by 42%, computed from only 48 matched correspondence pairs
against 250 in the reliable living-room case - and there the pipeline flags
the merge as not reliable, rather than silently returning a plausible-looking
room that might be wrong. For a listing that someone is going to book a stay
from, a method that knows and states when its own answer is shaky is worth
more, to me, than one that always returns a confident-looking number.

## Four bugs worth mentioning

Every one of these produced output that looked fine at a glance and was
wrong, which is the worst kind of bug in a reconstruction pipeline - nothing
crashes, nothing throws, the point cloud just isn't what it claims to be. I'm
listing them because "it looked plausible" turned out to be a genuinely
unreliable signal, and I'd rather be explicit about that than let this piece
imply the method worked cleanly the first time.

- An inverted colour-channel order left the point cloud mirrored -
  geometrically fine, but a left-right flip of the actual room.
- The floor detector could latch onto a wall instead of the floor, which is a
  silent failure: the reconstruction still "worked", it just oriented
  everything against the wrong plane.
- The initial rotation estimate that seeds the alignment was off by 167° on
  one pair - close enough to 180° that alignment never converged, because the
  optimiser was starting from almost the exact opposite of the right answer.
- Scale, for a stretch, simply wasn't being estimated at all - the pipeline
  was fusing point clouds at whatever arbitrary scale the depth model
  happened to produce for each one, which is the bug that motivated the
  two-estimate cross-check described above.

## What's still missing

The honest limit of the current method: each panorama is deformed
individually by the depth model - different distortions in different parts of
the same room - so no single rigid transform (rotation, translation, uniform
scale) can fully remove the alignment error. The roughly 8 cm of residual
misalignment on shared surfaces, mentioned above, is close to the floor of
what a rigid transform can achieve here. Getting past it means moving from one
rigid alignment per panorama pair to something piecewise, or genuinely
non-rigid - deforming different regions of the merged surface by different
amounts. That's the next piece of work, not a solved problem.

I want to be clear about where this sits: it's R&D. None of the four
approaches I've described - including this one - is anything I'd put in front
of a person deciding whether to book a villa, not yet. What I have is a
method that fills in real blind spots with real photographed geometry, tells
you when it isn't sure, and a clear list of what has to happen before it's
more than that.
